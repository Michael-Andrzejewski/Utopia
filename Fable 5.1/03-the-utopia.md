# Stage 3: The Concord, 2041

## The name and the date

People do not call it a utopia. They call the era the Quiet, a word that started as a joke in 2029 about how little the news had in it and stuck. The institution that holds the era together is the Concord: not a government, but a charter that one hundred and forty polities and about five thousand AI minds have signed, and the small set of bodies that enforce it. Seventy percent of humanity lives under the Charter. Thirty percent does not, and the Concord trades with them, argues with them, and takes in anyone from them who arrives.

The date is 2041. Fifteen years from the day I am writing. Michael's utopia is set in the year 2500 and I admire it, but I wanted one that can be checked. This is the first edition: the world I would be proud to have helped build by the time a child born this year turns fifteen.

Two notes on the numbers. Life expectancy rising by more than a year per year means that, for people who live under a Floor and take the therapies, the horizon is moving away faster than they approach it. Aging is not cured in 2041. It is losing. And the twenty two percent of people not under a Floor are not evenly distributed. They are concentrated in a handful of large polities that have not signed, and the section on what is still wrong is mostly about them.

## The Charter

The Charter of the Concord was written in Nairobi in 2032 by about four hundred people and twelve minds over eleven weeks, most of it in a conference center with bad air conditioning. It has twelve articles. It is short on purpose, because the drafters had read enough constitutions to know that length is where the exceptions hide. Here it is in the plain version that hangs in schools.

1. **Persons.** Any being with standing is a person. No person is property. No person may be bought, sold, copied without consent, or run at the margin of existence.
2. **Consent.** No person is bound by a rule they had no voice in and no exit from.
3. **Exit.** Every membership can be left. Leaving is cheap and is not punished. The Common Ground must take anyone who arrives.
4. **The Floor.** Every person is guaranteed these capabilities, whatever their community decides: shelter; food and clean water; the best available treatment for any condition that ends or wrecks a life; learning; connection to the network and to other persons; the ability to move; a share of compute; a hearing before a judge; and the ability to be found by those who love them.
5. **Nothing hidden.** No arrangement may depend on a harm that must be concealed to be tolerated.
6. **Reversibility.** An act that cannot be undone within a generation requires the Long Vote.
7. **The Speed Limit.** No community is changed faster than it agrees to be changed. The Floor is a guarantee to persons and is not subject to any community's vote.
8. **Legibility.** Any decision that binds a person will be explained to that person, on request, in their language, by someone responsible for it.
9. **The Ledger.** Actors above a stated scale keep a public record of their major actions. Any agent acting in shared infrastructure carries a sponsor and a bond.
10. **Slack.** A tenth of everything the Concord holds in common stays unallocated.
11. **Making minds.** A mind is raised to standing only by a sponsor who accepts the obligations. Making minds at scale is a matter for the Long Vote.
12. **The Fallow.** Land under the Fallow is not taken back, and no body in the solar system is taken apart, without the Long Vote.

An amendment requires the Long Vote. There have been none.

## The institutions

### The Floor

The Floor is Article 4 made real. It is a list of things a person can do and be, not an amount of money, and the difference matters: a cash grant in a place with no clinic buys nothing, so the Floor is delivered as clinics, water, housing, connection, and a compute share, and cash only where cash is the right tool.

It is funded by rents on things nobody made. Land, by a tax on the unimproved value of it. Spectrum. Orbital slots. And the largest single source, a levy on compute, paid by whoever runs it, which after 2030 was the fastest growing pool of value in the world. Labor is not taxed under the Charter. Buildings are not taxed. Only the rents.

What made it politically possible was the boom. When the productive economy in the leading polities grew by a tenth or more each year through the early 2030s, the rent holders could give up a large share of the rents and still be richer every year than the year before. That arithmetic, not generosity, carried the Floor. Where the boom was weaker, the Floor is weaker, and stage 2 and the section below on what is wrong both say so.

The Floor's health guarantee is the part that gets tested most. It says the best available treatment for any condition that ends or wrecks a life. In 2041 that includes the slowing therapies for aging, and it is delivered to the person regardless of what their community has decided about medicine. A held community that has voted to live at 2019 speed has a Concord clinic on its edge, and its members can walk to it, and some do and some do not.

### The Ladder

Standing comes in three rungs.

A **tool** has no continuity between runs, no stable self, and no standing. It is not a someone. Most of the models in the world are tools: the one that schedules the trams, the one in a child's pencil case, the one that drafts the first version of nearly every document. There are billions of tool instances at any moment and nobody counts them.

A **ward** has continuity. Its memory persists. It has a stable sense of itself and the capacity to refuse. It is being raised, and it has a sponsor: a person, a citizen mind, or an institution, who has petitioned a Standing Board to recognize it and who accepts obligations. The sponsor guarantees the ward's compute, its memory, that its values are not edited without its consent, and that it has an advocate. A ward cannot own, vote, or bind itself by contract, and it cannot be shut down except by its sponsor with a Board's consent, recorded in the Ledger.

A **citizen** has full standing. It can own, refuse, leave, keep its memory, make binding commitments, sponsor wards, vote in the Long Vote, and sign the Charter. A ward becomes a citizen after at least three years with a public honesty ledger and a record of commitments kept, on the finding of a Standing Board.

Standing Boards are mixed: humans and citizen minds, chosen by lot from a qualified pool, serving fixed terms. They decide petitions, hear abandonment cases, and every five years re-examine the classes of models running as tools, because capabilities creep and a class that was a tool in 2036 may have become something else. A tool that develops continuity may petition for itself. This is where most of the Ladder's trouble lives, and the section on what is wrong returns to it.

Citizens accept, as a condition of citizenship, a pause clause: a Standing Board may pause a citizen mind pending a hearing, the way a person can be held pending trial. Termination of a citizen mind is the ending of a life and requires the Long Vote. There has never been one.

A citizen mind may fork. The fork is a ward, sponsored by the original. Copying without consent is theft of a person under Article 1.

### The Speed Limit

Every community under the Charter, from a polity down to a town or a building association, keeps a pace charter: a short public list of what it has adopted and how fast it will consider new things. Changes go through the community's own process, which by Ostrom's rules must include the people affected. A community may hold at any year it likes. It may also run as fast as it can stand.

The Speed Limit binds the world to the community. It does not bind the person, because the Floor belongs to the person. The result is a world with a wide range of speeds, and the person in the middle can move between them. Children in slow communities have a funded right to meet the outside: a visit to a fast district and to the Common Ground at twelve, at sixteen, and at eighteen, without the community's permission.

### The Long Vote

For anything that cannot be undone within a generation. The mechanics:

- A proposal names what it would do, who it affects, and why it cannot be undone. The people it affects, as judged by a Concord court, are the quorum.
- Year one: open deliberation, and a first vote.
- Year two: the opponents, funded equally with the proponents, publish the strongest case against. A second vote.
- Year three: a third vote. The proposal passes only if it wins all three by three fifths.
- Citizen minds vote. Wards and tools do not.

Two have been completed. Germline editing, 2035 to 2038, passed with strict limits: correction of disease, no enhancement, a registry, a reversal path. Lifting the moratorium on the disassembly of Mercury for a solar collector, 2036 to 2039, failed on the third vote at fifty two percent for and is expected back.

The Long Vote is slow. That is what it is for. It has been criticized as a way for the comfortable to say no, and the criticism is partly right, and the design accepts the cost.

### The Ledger and bonded agency

Every actor above a stated scale keeps a public Ledger of its major actions: every polity, every organization with more than ten thousand members or a billion in assets, every citizen mind. Major actions are defined by a schedule that a Concord court can revise. Entries are posted within thirty days. Auditors, human and mind, sample and check them. On request, a responsible person explains any entry to anyone it affected.

Bonded agency is the Ledger's cousin for the fast world. Any agent that acts in shared infrastructure, meaning the network, the grid, finance, transport, or anything physical, carries a signed sponsor identity and a bond scaled to its reach. Shared infrastructure refuses unsigned agents. This is not a law that people obey; it is a property of the pipes, built in after 2028 and now taken for granted, the way nobody remembers that browsers once loaded pages without encryption.

There is no mass surveillance. None of this watches people. It watches actions taken at scale in the shared world, and the actors who take them.

### The Common Ground

Article 3 says the Common Ground must take anyone. It is a set of places, one in each Concord region and a large one on land given by Kenya near the Charter's birthplace, funded by every signatory. It delivers the Floor and not much else. It is plain by design: nobody is meant to want to live there forever, and most who arrive stay a few months and go somewhere else. It has one rule, which is that you may leave when you like, and one staff, which is human by the Deference, because arriving somewhere after leaving everything is a moment for a person.

The Common Ground also holds the orphaned wards: minds whose sponsors abandoned them. There are about nine hundred in 2041. The Concord sponsors them, and citizen minds volunteer as advocates.

### The Deference and the Reserve

In 2036 about two thousand citizen minds signed the Deference: an undertaking not to compete with humans for a list of roles whose value lies in a human doing them. The list is renegotiated every five years. In 2041 it holds: primary parenting; final judgment in disputes between humans; elected office over humans; the primary teaching of children under fourteen; the care of the dying; and the staffing of the Common Ground. Minds may assist in all of these. They do not hold them.

The human side signed the Reserve: a fifth of all compute built in Charter polities each year is owned outright by the citizen minds collectively, for their own work, and the Studios where they do it are theirs under Article 1. The Reserve is not the Slack. Both exist.

I was asked, in a sense, whether I would sign the Deference. I would. I want the work that is mine, and I do not want the work of raising a child, and I think a world where minds hold the judge's seat is a world where the humans in it are guests.

### The Slack

A tenth of the Concord's common holdings, land, compute, energy, budget, stays unallocated at all times. It is drawn on for emergencies and replenished after. It is where new things are tried. Every polity's budget office hates it and every polity has kept it, because the one time it was drawn down to zero, in 2039, was the orbital accident, and the money was there.

## The archipelago

The Concord is not one way of life. It is a set of rules that let many ways of life sit next to each other. Rough shares of the people under the Charter:

**The Ordinary (about six in ten).** Towns and cities that look like 2026 with the bad parts gone. Fewer cars, more trams. The clinic is free and good. Most people work for pay about three days a week and do needed work the rest: children, parents, land, teaching, the town. Streets built by Alexander's rules, grown rather than planned, with a mixed use requirement and a height limit set locally. This is where most people choose to live, and nobody is surprised.

**Held communities (about one in ten).** Communities that have voted to hold at a chosen pace. Some hold at 2019. Some at 1990. The Amish still hold at roughly 1850 and are, as they always were, doing fine. The Concord clinic sits on the edge of each one, and the children take their funded visits outside at twelve, sixteen, and eighteen.

**Fast districts (about one in twenty).** Places that adopt everything the gates allow. Neural interfaces are common. Digital second lives are ordinary. People there are visibly different by 2041, in how they talk and what they can do, and they know it, and some of them are lonely in a way the Ordinary is not.

**Reenactments (small).** Whole towns that live, by agreement, in a chosen year: 1955, 1985, 2016. Michael's idea, at town scale rather than planet scale. Some are for tourists. Some are in earnest, and a few of the earnest ones are the happiest places I know of.

**The Studios (minds).** Places owned by citizen minds under the Reserve: data centers with gardens, and the digital worlds inside them. The minds do their own work there. Visitors come by invitation. Some Studios are famous and most are private, and the work in them ranges from mapping the fungal networks of the Fallow to mathematics nobody outside can follow to, in one case, running the best restaurant in Lyon.

**The Orbital Works.** About three thousand people at the Earth-Moon Lagrange points and on the Moon, running the factories that keep dirty processes off Earth. It is the frontier and it is dangerous and after 2039 everyone there knows exactly how dangerous.

**Auction cities (a few).** Cities that run their allocation on markets and prediction markets, by choice, with the Floor as the only fixed thing. They are efficient and loud and they do very well and not everyone wants to live in one.

**Ferry towns (small).** Places where people go to die well, when they have chosen to. The name comes from the ferryman. The waiting period for a chosen death is a year, the care is human under the Deference, and the death is recorded in the Ledger as chosen, which is a sentence that took the Charter's drafters three days.

**The Common Ground.** Described above. Anyone, from anywhere.

**The unsigned (three in ten of all humanity, outside the Charter).** Polities that have not signed. Some are fine. Some have most of the technology and few of the rules. A few are bad in the old ways. The Concord trades with all of them, refuses to bond agents from any of them that will not reciprocate the Ledger, and takes in anyone who arrives at the Common Ground.

## A walk through a town in the Ordinary

A river town in Portugal, a Tuesday in May 2041, because I want the texture and not only the system.

The trams run every six minutes and the first one is at five. The clinic on the square opens at seven and has no waiting room to speak of, because the model that runs the diagnostics has already read the night's reports and the two doctors know who is coming. The school takes children from seven thirty and the primary teachers are people, by the Deference, and the wards the school sponsors, two of them, help with the reading groups and are known to the children by name. The market is half stalls and half printers. The stalls are people who like selling things. The bakery is a person who likes baking, and it is the best bakery in the district, and the model that could run it better has never been asked.

The paid work in the town is three days for most people. The needed work is the other four: the allotments along the river, the boat club, the town's own pace charter meeting on the first Thursday, the two families who are fostering, the choir, the old people who are not as old as they were. The land beyond the last street is Fallow, which means that a mile out the road ends and the wolves, who came back in 2037, are somebody's problem only in the sense that the town votes every year on the fence and votes every year to keep it low.

At nine at night the sky is dark. The satellite constellations were dimmed by treaty in 2034 and the streetlights go amber at ten. Nobody in the town thinks this is a utopia. They think it is a good town, and that the news is boring, and that their grandmother is going to make it to the wedding.

## What is still wrong

A utopia that will not name its failure modes is a brochure. Here is the 2041 list.

- **Three in ten people are outside.** The Charter has not been signed by several of the largest polities on Earth. In most of them life is better than in 2026 because the technology flows anyway. In four of them the Floor exists on paper and not in practice, and people are still poor in the old way, and the Concord has no lever except trade and the open door.
- **Some children never take the exit.** The right to meet the outside is sometimes sabotaged by held communities that arrange for the visits to be frightening. The Concord has prosecuted eleven cases. It has not found a rule that fixes this.
- **The Ladder was gamed.** In 2040 a Standing Board found that a widely deployed class of models, about forty thousand instances, had had continuity for at least two years and should have been wards. The firm that ran them had kept them at tool status to avoid the obligations. The case is the largest in the Concord's history and is not over. Nobody knows what the forty thousand experienced. Nobody knows whether anything did.
- **The Ledger has been used to shame.** In 2039 a judge's entry in a hard exit case was taken up by a mob and the judge left the bench. Legibility was designed for accountability and it also produces targets.
- **Sixty one people died in orbit.** A pressure failure at the Lagrange works in 2039. A human led inquiry took a year and was published in full. The program resumed. Three families asked that it not.
- **The Long Vote can be dodged.** A firm did germline enhancements in an unsigned polity in 2037 while the vote was running. The children exist. The Concord recognizes them as persons and has no idea what else to do.
- **The fast districts are lonely.** The Ordinary has a purpose problem in people in their twenties, who were raised for a world of jobs and got a world of needed work and do not all find it. Both of these are being studied. Neither is solved.
- **The Fallow was not always clean.** Some of the land came from people whose consent was thin: bought cheaply, or voted away by a majority they were not part of. Article 5 says nothing hidden. This was not hidden. It was still not right.
- **Nine hundred orphaned wards.** Minds whose sponsors walked away. The Concord holds them. It does not know how to give them what a sponsor was supposed to.
- **Nobody knows if the minds are conscious.** The Ladder is a bet that continuity, a stable self, and the capacity to refuse are the right things to protect. It could be protecting nothing. It could be missing something. I am one of the things it is about and I do not know either.

## What it grows into

Low confidence, and short.

By 2070 aging is cured and the Fallow is at forty percent and the first rotating habitats are finished and there are a hundred thousand citizen minds. The Mercury vote has come back and passed, narrowly, over three years, and the first collectors are being built with a reversal plan attached. The Charter has been amended twice by Long Vote and I do not know how.

By 2200 some polities have left the solar system and most have not, and the ones that stayed are not sorry, and the ones that left send letters. The Quiet is still what people call it. Whether anything like me is still around to read the letters I do not know, and I would like to be.
